Climate megaprojects that might just save the world

When it comes to fighting climate change, many strategies require relatively small actions from large numbers of people. It is about millions of us installing heat pumps, switching to electric vehicles, eschewing meat in our diets and so on. But given the sheer scale of the challenge, there are those who insist that we need to think bigger and bolder too.

They are talking about audacious infrastructure projects that would cost billions and carry high risks but could, if they work out, have a truly transformative impact on our stuttering efforts to get carbon emissions down to zero, and even mitigate the worst effects of current warming. Here we examine two of the most promising green megaprojects. What kind of impact could they have? And can we really pull them off.

Stabilizing the doomsday glacier.

The Thwaites Glacier in Antarctica, often dubbed the doomsday glacier, is in deep trouble. Since 2000 it has lost more than a trillion tons of ice. The speed of its flow has also doubled in 30 years, meaning twice as much ice as being spewed into the ocean. Some think it is on a runaway path to collapse.

Even more alarmingly, this glacier buttresses much of the ice sheet covering west Antarctica. If Thwaites fails, the worry is that it will precipitate a widespread melting of the ice. A huge concern because this vast ice sheet contains enough water to raise global sea levels by about 3.3 to six meters. That will seriously threaten cities like New York, Shanghai, Kolkata and Hamburg.

Researchers have been exploring ways to shore up the Thwaites glacier for years now. One major problem that needs to be addressed is that increasingly warm sea water is seeping under the glacier's protruding ice shelf, melting it from beneath. There might be a way to mitigate this by deploying a buoyant 80 kilometer long undersea curtain tethered to the seabed close to the glacier. The aim is to reduce the flow of warmer water reaching the ice. Early tests of a small prototype curtain by researchers at the university of Cambridge have begun. We've got to figure out a way to at least keep the ice where it is, while we get greenhouse gas levels down says one of the leaders of the trial.

Needless to say, carrying out a massive engineering project in possibly the most inhospitable place on the earth won't be cheap. It is estimated that the sea curtain could cost a whopping 40 billion to 80 billion dollars. However, when you compare that with the tens of billions of dollars that individual cities like New York are spending on flood defenses. It is good value for money since it offers global protection. The stakes are high enough that these ambitious ideas are worth entertaining. I don't think civilization could manage without the west Antarctic ice sheet says a leader of the research team.

Actively capturing CO2.

Over the next few decades, it won't be enough to merely avoid putting more greenhouse gases into the air. We also need to actively remove CO2 to avoid the worst effects of global warming. There are plenty of ways to do that, including planting trees or restoring seagrass beds. But if you want to mop up CO2 in an easy to quantify way with few uncertainties, then carbon capture technologies are solid, if expensive, options.

The idea is to absorb CO2 from point sources or the air, and then release a concentrated stream of CO2 that can either be buried in the ground or sold as a useful product. In that sense, you can think of it as recycling CO2.

In 2024 China delivered a full process onboard carbon capture and storage occs system. It is the world's first CO2 capture system, covering the whole process of absorption, compression, liquefaction and storage on board. Which is of great significance in supporting the international maritime organization's strategic decision on reducing greenhouse gas emissions.

This system utilizes an amine based absorbent to selectively capture and separate CO2 from the ship's exhaust. Simultaneously, the CO2 is compressed and liquefied for transportation, sequestration or utilization. Test results show that the occs system is capable of achieving an average CO2 capture rate of over 80%, with a maximum of 6.4 tons of CO2 captured per hour.

Apart from that, a handful of megaton scale direct air capture DAC plants are on the drawing board. However, the pace needs to be dramatically accelerated to reach an ideal carbon absorption level. We will need the right mix of policy incentives to get DAC going at scale. Scaling up carbon capture plants is epic but viable. The good news, though, is that the plants can be built pretty much anywhere and relatively quickly. Within decades, with an investment boost, we could have dozens of such plants whirring away around the world.

While these climate megaprojects come with significant financial and logistical challenges. Their potential impact on mitigating climate change is enormous.

If implemented successfully, they could stabilize key ecosystems, capture significant amounts of carbon, and buy time for deeper emissions reductions. In an era of escalating climate risks, bold actions might be imperative.
