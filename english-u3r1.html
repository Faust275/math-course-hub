Fusion energy fuels optimism for the future

Fusion energy is perhaps the longest of long shots. To build a fusion reactor is essentially to create an artificial star. Scientists have been studying the physics of fusion for about a century and working to harness the process for decades. Despite the challenges it poses, fusion has enormous potential. It could safely provide an immense and steady torrent of electricity by harnessing abundant fuel made from sea water to ignite the same reaction that powers the sun. It would produce no greenhouse gases and minimal waste compared to conventional energy sources.

People are well aware of the power of nuclear fission, which is what happens when big atoms like uranium and plutonium split apart and release energy. These reactions powered the very first atomic bombs, and today they power conventional nuclear reactors.

Fusion is even more potent. It's what happens when light atomic nuclei stick together, fusing to create heavier ones and releasing energy. The most common form is two hydrogen atoms fusing to create a helium atom.

The reason that fusion generates so much energy is that the new nucleus weighs a smidgen less than the sum of the parts that formed it. That tiny bit of lost matter is converted into energy. According to the famous formula, E = mc2. E stands for energy and m stands for mass. The last part of the formula C, a constant that measures the speed of light about 300,000 kilometers per second, which is then squared. So there's an enormous multiplier for the matter that's converted into energy, making fusion an extraordinarily powerful reaction.

These basics are well understood, and researchers are confident that it's possible to harness fusion to power our planet. But so far, it's been elusive.

For a demonstration of fusion, one only has to look up at the sun during the day. Even from about 93 million miles away, our nearest star generates enough energy to heat up the earth through the vacuum of space. But the sun has an advantage that we don't have here on the earth. It is very, very big. One of the difficulties with fusion is that atomic nuclei, the positively charged cores of atoms normally repel each other. To overcome that repulsion and spark fusion, we have to get the atoms moving really fast in a confined space, which makes collisions more likely.

A star like the sun, which is about 333,000 times the mass of the earth, generates gravity that accelerates atoms towards its centre, heating them up, confining them and igniting fusion. The fusion reactions then provide the energy to speed up other atomic nuclei and trigger even more fusion reactions.

Imitating the sun on the earth is a tall order. Humans have been able to trigger fusion, but in ways that are uncontrolled, like in thermonuclear weapons sometimes called hydrogen bombs. Controlled fusion has been demonstrated in laboratories, but usually under conditions that consume far more energy than the reaction produces. While certain experiments have achieved net energy gains temporarily. Sustaining a fusion reaction with a net energy gain over a longer period remains a challenge. Besides, the reaction generally requires creating and maintaining a high energy state of matter known as plasma, which has quirks and behaviors that scientists are still trying to understand.

To harness fusion as an energy source, scientists need to trigger it in a controlled way that yields far more energy than they put in. Teams around the world are studying different ways to accomplish this. But the approaches tend to fall into two broad categories. One involves using magnets to contain the plasma. This is the approach used by China's HL-3 tokamak. In 2023 the magnetic field structure achieved a successful high confinement operation under a plasma current of 1 million amperes, demonstrating a significant improvement in the operational capabilities of China's magnetic confinement fusion facilities. The other category involves confining the fusion fuel and compressing it in a tiny space with the aid of lasers. This is the approach used by the National Ignition Facility (NIF) at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in California, US.

Replicating a star requires doing this research at massive scales, so fusion experiments often involve the most powerful scientific instruments ever built. The central solenoid of the international thermonuclear experimental reactor iter, the world's largest fusion project, for example, can generate a magnetic force strong enough to hoist an aircraft carrier six feet out of the water.

Building hardware to withstand such extreme conditions is its own scientific and engineering challenge. Managing such massive experiments has also been a struggle. According to its 2001 design, the initial estimated construction cost for the ITER project was 5 billion Euros. Now the cost has far exceeded the estimate and is still rising.

An upside to the intricacy of fusion reactions, is that it is almost impossible to cause a runaway reaction or meltdown of the sort that has devastated nuclear power plants like Chernobyl. If a fusion reactor is disrupted, the reaction rapidly fizzles out. In addition, the main waste product of hydrogen fusion is helium, an inert gas. The process can induce some reactor materials to become radioactive. But the radioactivity is much lower and the quantity of hazardous waste is far smaller than those produced by conventional nuclear power plants. So nuclear fusion energy could become one of the safest sources of electricity.

The journey toward fusion energy has yielded benefits for other fields, particularly in plasma physics, which is used extensively in manufacturing semiconductors for electronics. As put by one fusion researcher. Plasma processing is one of the things that make your smartphones possible.

Artificial star power might not illuminate the world for decades. But the foundations have to be laid now through research, development and deployment. It may very well become humanity's crowning achievement more than a century in the making.
