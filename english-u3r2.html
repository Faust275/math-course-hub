Is geothermal power heating up as an energy source?

Glistening in the dry expanses of the Nevada desert is an unusual kind of power plant that harnesses energy not from the sun or wind, but from the earth itself.

The geothermal project, known as Project Red, pumps water thousands of feet into the ground down where rocks are hot enough to roast a turkey. Around the clock the structure sucks the heated water back up. It is then used to power generators. Since November 2024 this carbon-free, earth-born power has been flowing onto a local grid in Nevada.

Geothermal energy, though continuously radiating from the earth's super hot core, has long been a relatively niche source of electricity, largely limited to volcanic regions. But geothermal enthusiasts dream of sourcing the earth's power in places without such specific geological conditions, like Project Red's Nevada site.

Such next-generation geothermal systems have been in the works for decades, but they've proven expensive and technologically difficult, and have sometimes even triggered earthquakes. Some experts hope that newer efforts like Project Red may now finally signal a turning point, by leveraging techniques that were developed in oil and gas extraction to improve reliability and cost efficiency.

The advances have garnered hopes that with enough time and money, geothermal power, which currently generates less than one per cent of the world's electricity, could become a mainstream clean energy source.

Some posit that geothermal energy could be a valuable tool in transitioning off of fossil fuels and reducing greenhouse gas emissions. For instance, every year China's Yangbajing geothermal power station can help cut 420,000 tons of carbon dioxide emissions. And save 116,000 tons of standard coal. Moreover, geothermal energy can provide a continuous backup for intermittent clean energy sources like solar and wind.

Geothermal energy works best with two things heat plus rocks that are permeable enough to carry water. In places where molten rock sizzles close to the surface, water will seep through porous volcanic rocks, warm up and bubble upward as hot water, steam, or both. If the water or steam is hot enough, ideally at least around 300 degrees Fahrenheit, it can be extracted from the ground and used to power generators for electricity.

There's a huge amount of heat in the earth, and some natural geothermal resources remain untapped. But much of the geothermal energy is locked inside the rocks that aren't permeable, pushing experts to consider ways of extracting geothermal energy from areas where the energy is much harder to access.

Tapping that heat requires deep drilling and creating cracks in these non volcanic, dense rocks to allow water to flow through. Since the 1970s, engineers have been developing enhanced geothermal systems, e GS that do just that. Applying methods similar to hydraulic fracturing or fracking, used to suck oil and gas out of deep rocks. Water is pumped at high pressure into the injection wells up to several miles deep to blast cracks into the rocks. The cracked rocks and water create an underground radiator where water heats before rising to the surface through the extraction wells.

Dozens of such EGS installations have been built in the US, Europe, Australia, the Republic of Korea, Japan, ETC. Most of them experimental and government funded with mixed success. Famously, one EGS plant in the Republic of Korea was abruptly shuttered in 2017 after having probably caused a 5.5 magnitude earthquake. Fracking of any kind can add pressure to nearby tectonic faults. Other issues were technological. Some plants didn't create enough fractures for good heat exchange, or fractures traveled in the wrong direction and failed to connect the wells.

Luckily, solutions exist for both safety and technological problems. There are in fact, robust protocols for avoiding earthquakes, such as not drilling near active faults. Long term monitoring of the operating EGS plants In France and Germany has documented only minor tremors. Building confidence in the safety of the technology. Importantly, drilling and fracking methodology has improved by leaps and bounds thanks to the boom in oil and gas extraction from shale rocks that began in the 2010s.

For instance, several techniques from the shale industry have been applied to Project Red. Engineers drilled almost 8000 feet downward into the Nevada rocks, where the temperatures reached nearly 380 degrees Fahrenheit, and then at the bottom drilled another 3250 foot horizontal well to expand the area of hot rocks that the system could touch. A technique used in oil and gas extraction in order to maximize yield. The company also fractured the surrounding rocks at several sites along the horizontal well to create a more extensive web of cracks for water to trickle through. Technologically speaking, compared to earlier EGS efforts, they are a big step forward.

There are other types of geothermal technology as well. Some companies are exploring the feasibility of extracting super hot rock geothermal energy. Essentially a young, extreme variant of EGS that involves drilling down even deeper into the earth's crust to a depth where water reaches a supercritical, vapor-like state that allows it to carry much more energy than either steam or liquid.

In short, it's an important moment for geothermal energy, and not just for providing carbon-free electricity. Geothermal brine hauled out of the earth is rich in lithium and other critical minerals that can be used to build green technologies like solar panels and EV batteries. There's also a growing push to use geothermal heat directly to warm buildings, either through shallow heat pumps for residential buildings or larger systems designed for entire districts.

As one geologist said, our earth is geothermal, and so we owe it to ourselves to do everything we can to use it.
